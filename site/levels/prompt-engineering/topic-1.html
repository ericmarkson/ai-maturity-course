Level 3 — Prompt Engineering

Why Some Prompts Work and Others Fail (How the Model Reads You)

Learning objective: Connect Level 1's next-piece prediction model to everyday prompting—explaining why vague prompts drift toward generic "average internet" slop or confident hallucinations, and how clear details remove guesswork.

Definition

A language model does not read your mind or look up what you meant—it predicts the most likely continuation of your exact text, one word-piece at a time. Prompt clarity means removing unnecessary degrees of freedom (unstated audience, goal, scope, evidence, or shape) so the right answer becomes the highest-probability continuation.

How It Works (Next-Piece Prediction Meets Ambiguity)

Remember Level 1: after reading your prompt, the model scores thousands of possible next tokens and picks a plausible continuation. Every detail you leave blank stays a wide-open probability cloud, causing three common failures:

  • Unstated audience or goal → "Average internet" fluff: If you type "Write a marketing email," the model blends millions of generic promos it saw in training. You get clichés like "Unlock your potential in today's fast-paced world!" because generic filler is the safest statistical average.
  • Unstated scope or criteria → Mismatched depth: Asking "Compare cloud providers" could mean a 10-page enterprise architecture review or a $20/month hobby hosting chart. Without scope and comparison axes, the model guesses a shallow middle ground that fits nobody well.
  • Missing source facts or boundaries → Confident hallucination: If you ask about your company's refund policy without pasting the policy (and without telling the model to say "I don't know" when facts are missing), next-piece prediction smoothly completes the sentence with realistic-sounding numbers like "30-day full refund"—even if your real policy is 14 days.

Every concrete detail you supply—who is reading, what decision they need to make, which facts to trust, and what cannot be invented—locks down another degree of freedom and collapses the guesswork.

Worked Example: Side-by-Side Vague vs. High-Signal Prompt

Vague Prompt (High Degrees of Freedom) 
Summarize our Q3 ops update.

Typical output: Three generic paragraphs restating every minor detail equally—or inventing fake Q3 numbers if you forgot to paste the update.

High-Signal Prompt (Tight Constraints) 
Audience: VP of Ops deciding next week's hiring freeze.
Using ONLY the notes in <notes>...</notes>, list the top 3 delivery bottlenecks and their dollar impact in 3 bullets (≤15 words each). If dollar impact isn't stated, write "Impact not listed."

Result: Crisp, scannable, grounded executive bullets with zero made-up figures.

Common Pitfall: Prompting Like a Search Engine

Years of using Google trained us to type 3–5 keywords ("fix python bug" or "refund policy email") and scan ten blue links ourselves. A search engine retrieves existing pages; an AI generates a brand-new reply shaped entirely by your prompt. Treating a prompt box like a keyword bar forces the model to invent every missing requirement on your behalf.
