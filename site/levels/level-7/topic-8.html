Scaling Laws

Learning objective: The learner can read a loss-vs-compute scaling chart and explain what it implies about whether to add more parameters or more training data for a fixed compute budget.

Definition

Scaling laws are empirical relationships showing how model performance improves in a predictable way as model size, dataset size, and compute all increase together. They let researchers predict a large model's loss before fully training it, and reveal that for any fixed compute budget there's a "compute-optimal" split between how many parameters to use and how much data to train on.

How It Works

  • The Power-Law Loss Relation: Pretraining cross-entropy loss follows an empirical power law L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ, where N is parameter count, D is training tokens, E is the irreducible entropy of natural text (~1.69), and dense transformer training compute scales as roughly C ≈ 6 · N · D FLOPs (2 FLOPs per parameter forward, 4 backward).
  • Chinchilla Compute-Optimal IsoFLOP Frontier: Sweeping (N, D) pairs along fixed-compute curves (isoFLOPs), Hoffmann et al. (2022) showed that α ≈ 0.34 and β ≈ 0.28 are nearly balanced—so for minimum training loss at fixed compute C, N and D should scale roughly proportionally (~20 tokens per parameter). Many earlier large models (like 280B Gopher on 300B tokens) were heavily parameter-bloated and undertrained.
  • Training-Optimal vs. Inference-Optimal Scaling: Chinchilla minimizes training FLOPs only. Because serving cost scales with N across billions of queries, production teams routinely over-train smaller models far past 20:1 (e.g., training an 8B model on 15T tokens) to win massive inference latency and serving savings.

Worked Example

Compare two runs spending the exact same pretraining budget C ≈ 5.88 × 1023 FLOPs (6 · N · D):

  • Run A (Chinchilla-style, 70B × 1.4T tokens): 20 tokens/param—sits directly on the compute-optimal frontier with lower loss and 4× cheaper serving.
  • Run B (Undertrained giant, 280B × 350B tokens): Only 1.25 tokens/param—the B / Dβ data term stays elevated, leaving Run B with higher loss despite identical training FLOPs and 4× heavier deployment VRAM.

Common Pitfall

Treating 20:1 as a hard law for serving or assuming clean scaling continues on stale data. First, if your model will handle heavy inference traffic, training a smaller model on far more tokens beats the Chinchilla training-optimal split on total lifecycle cost. Second, repeating low-quality or duplicated synthetic tokens degrades the effective D−β slope, flattening returns faster than power-law curves predict on fresh, diverse data.
