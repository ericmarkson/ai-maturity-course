What a Neural Network Is, Conceptually

Learning objective: Explain, using a relay-committee analogy and zero equations, how a neural network turns raw inputs into a decision by blending weighted signals across layers.

Definition

A neural network is a system of simple signal-combining units ("artificial neurons") wired together in layers: an input layer that reads clues, one or more hidden layers that mix those clues into intermediate patterns, and an output layer that delivers the final answer. Learning happens by adjusting the strength (weight) of every connection until guesses match real examples.

How It Works (The Relay Committee Analogy)

Imagine a three-stage team judging whether an audio-photo clue is a cat or a dog:

  • Input layer (Spotters): Spotter 1 measures ear pointiness; Spotter 2 measures vocal pitch; Spotter 3 measures tail wag speed.
  • Hidden layer (Pattern Judges): Each judge listens to all three spotters—giving big trust (high positive weight) to clues they care about, ignoring irrelevant ones (near-zero weight), or counting clues against an idea (negative weight)—and sends a combined hunch forward.
  • Output layer (Final Vote): Two final captains weigh the judges' hunches and light up whichever score is higher: CAT or DOG.

Common Pitfall: Taking "Brain-Inspired" Too Literally

Artificial neurons are loosely inspired by how biological brain cells send impulses across synapses, yet they are vastly simpler—just weighted arithmetic sums stored on silicon, with no feelings, consciousness, or living chemistry. Think of them as layered dimmer switches, not miniature human brains.
