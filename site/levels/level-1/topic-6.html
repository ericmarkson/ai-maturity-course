Basic AI Ethics: Bias and Privacy

Learning objective: Trace how skewed training data leads an AI to produce biased decisions even without malicious code, weigh everyday privacy trade-offs, and explain why human builders remain accountable for AI outcomes.

Definition

Two real-world risks show up even when an AI model has zero software bugs: algorithmic bias (an AI faithfully reproducing unfairness or gaps present in its training data) and privacy exposure (personal voice, location, health, or work data collected and retained to power smarter features). Neither makes "the AI" responsible—human design and deployment choices always govern accountability.

How It Works (The Mirror Analogy)

Think of an ML model as a high-speed mirror trained on yesterday's records:

  • Bias from skewed history: If a hiring tool learns from 10 years of past hires where one group was historically overlooked, the model spots traits common among past hires and scores qualified applicants from underrepresented groups lower—without anyone programming prejudice explicitly.
  • Bias from missing representation: Facial recognition trained mostly on well-lit photos of light-skinned faces misses darker-skinned faces or varied lighting far more often.
  • Privacy trade-offs: Smarter personalization (voice assistants, smart keyboards, medical tools) often runs by sending personal audio, text, or behavior logs to cloud servers—creating tension between convenience and keeping sensitive data private.

Common Pitfall: "We Deleted the Gender/Race Column, So Our Model Is Unbiased"

Even if you strip out sensitive columns directly, models quickly pick up proxy clues—like zip codes, college clubs, or phrasing patterns—that correlate with demographics in the training data. True fairness checks audit actual outcome rates across groups and keep humans accountable rather than hiding behind "the algorithm decided it."
