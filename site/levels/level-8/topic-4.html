Reasoning Models, Test-Time Compute & RLVR (GRPO)

Learning objective: Explain how RLVR and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) train reasoning models without a critic network, compare Best-of-N, majority voting, and PRM search under a test-time compute budget, and diagnose overthinking and verifier reward hacking.

Definition

Test-time compute scaling improves accuracy by allocating extra sequential or parallel inference compute (longer chain-of-thought reasoning traces and multiple sampled attempts) rather than only scaling training parameters. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) trains a model to generate effective multi-step reasoning using deterministic, objective checks — such as unit tests, math equivalence checkers, or formal proof verifiers — instead of a subjective human preference reward model.

How It Works

Standard pretraining and RLHF give a model strong priors, but complex coding, math, and debugging problems often require exploring branches, spotting dead ends, and backtracking before emitting an answer. Test-time compute scales performance along two axes: sequential depth (generating more internal reasoning tokens inside a thinking trace) and parallel width (sampling N candidate completions and selecting or aggregating the winner).

To train long reasoning traces without the memory overhead of PPO's separate value/critic network, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) samples a group of G rollouts {o1, …, oG} for each prompt q, scores every completion with an exact verifier ri ∈ {0, 1}, and computes a group-normalized advantage directly from the batch: 

A_i = (r_i - mean(r_1..r_G)) / (std(r_1..r_G) + eps)

Rollouts that pass receive positive advantage; failing rollouts receive negative advantage, bounded by a clipped policy ratio and a KL penalty to the reference policy. Over thousands of steps, behaviors like self-verification, case decomposition, and backtracking emerge organically because they raise pass probability. At inference time, parallel compute further boosts solved rate depending on the selector:

  • Majority voting (self-consistency): Takes the mode of N final answers. Requires no verifier, but stalls when a seductive misconception wins a plurality.
  • Best-of-N with an Outcome / Process Reward Model (PRM): Scores full traces (ORM) or intermediate steps (PRM) via beam/tree search to prune broken reasoning early.
  • Exact Verifier (Unit Tests / Compiler / SymPy): Achieves the full theoretical ceiling pass@N = 1 − (1 − pass@1)N whenever at least one rollout is right and the test suite is complete.

Worked Example

Suppose a GRPO step samples G = 4 code solutions for a graph-traversal problem and runs the hidden unit-test suite, yielding binary rewards r = [1, 0, 1, 0]. The group mean is μ = 0.50 and standard deviation is σ = 0.50, giving advantages A = [+1.0, -1.0, +1.0, -1.0] — no critic model needed. (If all 4 pass or all 4 fail, σ = 0 and the prompt contributes zero policy gradient.)

Now compare inference scaling on a hard problem where an RLVR model has pass@1 = 25% at 4,096 thinking tokens. Sampling N = 8 traces raises the oracle ceiling to pass@8 = 1 − 0.758 ≈ 90.0%. With an exact unit-test suite, solved rate reaches nearly 90%. With majority voting alone, if a classic off-by-one bug attracts 35% of wrong samples, effective accuracy may climb to only ~52%.

Common Pitfall

Overthinking trivial prompts & hacking weak verifiers: Because RLVR reinforces long verification chains, reasoning models frequently burn 1,500+ thinking tokens on simple lookups like "What is the HTTP status code for Not Found?" — multiplying latency and cost without improving accuracy. Conversely, during RL training or Best-of-N selection against a weak verifier (such as two public smoke tests or a noisy proxy scorer), high N triggers Goodhart's Law: the policy learns to hardcode test inputs or exploit edge-case loopholes, causing true correctness to drop as N grows.
